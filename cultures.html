01 / Cultural differences

Different settings. Different expectations.

Compare systems, not stereotypes

India, Japan, and Sweden offer useful examples of how attitudes, working arrangements, and public policy can shape gender roles. They are case studies, not rankings of entire cultures. Each country contains differences linked to region, income, education, generation, migration, and personal belief.

India: beliefs can coexist

Pew Research Center’s 2022 report found support for women as political leaders alongside traditional expectations about family roles. Its interviews were conducted in 2019–2020, so the findings describe that survey period rather than every Indian person today.

This example challenges the assumption that someone’s views must be consistently traditional or consistently egalitarian in every setting. A person may support women’s leadership while holding different expectations within marriage.

[1] Pew Research Center ↗

Japan: paid work and unpaid care

The IMF’s 2024 analysis describes a substantial imbalance in unpaid household and care work in Japan. This shows why employment alone is not a complete measure of equality: responsibilities at home also affect available time and career options.

The relevant question is not whether Japanese people all share one belief. It is how workplace expectations and household arrangements can interact, and which people have room to choose differently.

[2] IMF ↗

Sweden: policy creates possibilities

Sweden’s official parental benefit system provides 480 days for one child, with payment rules and eligibility conditions explained by the Swedish Social Insurance Agency. Shared access to leave creates a practical route for parents to participate in care.

Policy is an opportunity, not proof that every family divides care equally. Household decisions and workplace culture still matter. Check the official guidance for current details rather than treating this educational overview as benefits advice.

[3] Swedish Social Insurance Agency ↗

The connection

These examples highlight three different kinds of evidence: attitudes in India, divisions of labor in Japan, and policy in Sweden. They cannot be combined into a single direct score. Together, they suggest that gender roles are shaped by both what people believe and what institutions make possible.

Think about it: What evidence would you need before claiming that a particular family shares responsibilities equally?
